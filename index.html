How the model works

Lap model. Quasi-steady-state, sampled every ~4 m along the line. Corner speed comes from the car's lateral limit at that speed; a forward pass adds power and traction limits, a backward pass adds braking, and a friction ellipse ties cornering to longitudinal grip.

Vehicle. Each axle carries its own share of the cornering force, so the limit is whichever axle saturates first (front means understeer, rear means oversteer). Lateral load transfer is split between axles by roll stiffness, downforce by aero balance, and braking force by a fixed bias. Tyres lose grip coefficient as load rises, so every load transfer costs grip. The car's width is subtracted from the usable road.

Optimiser. The line is a cubic spline of lateral offsets. Lap time is differentiated exactly, by a hand-written reverse pass through the whole lap model. L-BFGS then runs coarse to fine: 48 m knots with the lap model's hard limits blurred, down to 6 m knots on the exact model, followed by a short local polish.

Combined grip. A GGV table gives, for each speed and cornering load, the most braking or drive that keeps both axles inside their own friction circles, with load transfer, brake bias and the driven axle(s) included. Each 4 m step respects the more heavily loaded of its two ends.

Yaw. Changing yaw rate needs a yaw moment, which only spare grip on one axle can supply: the front when tightening into a turn, the rear when unwinding. That becomes an effective curvature, |k| + l·|dk/ds|, checked against that axle's limit, where l comes from wheelbase, weight distribution and yaw inertia.

Powertrain. Electric: one fixed reduction, full torque up to a base speed, constant power above it, and a cut at the motor's maximum speed. Combustion: an empirical engine power curve shaped by where torque peaks, through geometrically spaced gears; the best gear is used at every speed.

Dynamic check. A time-stepped bicycle model with Pacejka-style tyres, load-transfer lag and a steering-rate limit drives the line. Its driver applies the steering and drive force from the model solved backwards along the line, plus small corrections. A bisection finds the highest fraction of the lap model's speeds it completes on track without sliding. The driver isn't perfect, so treat the margin as a lower bound good to about half a percent.

The 3D body motion, steering and wheel spin are cosmetic, not part of the physics model.

Not modelled yet. Tyre temperature and wear, battery or thermal derating, track camber and elevation, aero changes with ride height.
